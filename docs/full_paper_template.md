## Abstract

The scratch-wound healing assay is a cornerstone of *in vitro* cell migration studies, yet automated quantification remains limited by single-frame independence, training-data requirements, and the absence of biological constraints in existing tools. Here we present **Quantification**, a fully automated computational pipeline that segments wound boundaries from phase-contrast time-lapse microscopy using local variance texture analysis and enforces a biologically motivated monotonic closure constraint across the temporal sequence. Unlike deep-learning approaches, Quantification requires no annotated training data and operates on standard phase-contrast images in $O(HW)$ time per frame. The pipeline comprises a seven-stage single-frame detector --- local variance mapping, Y-band detection, column-wise edge extraction, RANSAC-based outlier rejection, Savitzky--Golay smoothing, vectorized mask reconstruction, and geometric validation --- followed by a stateful temporal integrator that constrains per-column edges to move only inward over time. We validated Quantification on 325 baseline images from two independent scratch-wound experiments in immortalized skeletal muscle cell lines (iMC ISOR544C and iMC MUTR544C) treated with candesartan (AT1R blocker), an ALK5 inhibitor (TGF-$\beta$ receptor I antagonist), vehicle (DMSO), or media control across three concentrations (0.01--1.0 mM). Expert manual review confirmed 98.9% detection accuracy on 285 wound-bearing images, with a mean Dice coefficient of 0.91 against hand-drawn ground truth (n = 5). The automatic quality-control algorithm achieved perfect sensitivity (1.00) for wound-bearing images. Downstream analysis of 282 validated trajectories revealed modest wound closure across all conditions (15.5--20.0% at 24 h), with ALK5 inhibition producing the lowest fractional closure (15.5 $\pm$ 0.8%), consistent with TGF-$\beta$-mediated suppression of collective cell migration. No condition reached 50% closure within the 24-hour observation window. The pipeline processes a typical 20-frame trajectory in under 10 seconds on commodity hardware and is released as open-source Python code with fully vectorized NumPy operations and configurable parallel processing. Quantification addresses a critical methodological gap by combining training-free segmentation with physics-informed temporal constraints, producing smooth, strictly non-increasing area trajectories that are directly interpretable without post-hoc smoothing.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Scratch-Wound Assay

The scratch-wound (or wound-healing) assay is one of the most widely used *in vitro* methods for studying collective cell migration (Liang et al., 2007; Jonkman et al., 2014; Grada et al., 2017). A confluent cell monolayer is mechanically disrupted --- typically by drawing a pipette tip across the surface --- creating a cell-free gap. Time-lapse microscopy then records the progressive closure of this gap as cells at the wound margin proliferate and migrate inward. The rate and completeness of wound closure serve as proxies for migratory capacity and are routinely used to evaluate drug effects, gene knockdowns, and phenotypic differences between cell lines (Yarrow et al., 2004; Ashby and Zijlstra, 2012; Pinto et al., 2023). The assay's simplicity, low cost, and compatibility with high-content screening platforms have made it a workhorse of cell biology for over two decades (Stamm et al., 2016).

### 1.2 Quantification Remains a Bottleneck

Despite the assay's conceptual simplicity, quantification remains a significant bottleneck. Manual measurement of wound area from exported images introduces inter-observer variability, is labor-intensive --- especially for multi-well, multi-timepoint experiments --- and does not scale to the hundreds of trajectories generated by modern high-content screening platforms. Several computational tools have been proposed, including TScratch (Gebaeck et al., 2009), ImageJ plugins (Suarez-Arnedo et al., 2020), the Robust Quantitative Scratch Assay (Vargas et al., 2016), AIM (Nunes Viana et al., 2017), CellProfiler pipelines (McQuin et al., 2018), and more recently level-set methods with spatial sector analysis (Martinotti et al., 2025). However, they share common limitations:

**Single-frame independence.** Most tools process each image in isolation, ignoring the temporal structure of the experiment and producing noisy area-versus-time curves with non-biological fluctuations --- apparent wound re-opening that contradicts the physical reality of the assay (Treloar and Simpson, 2013). This is particularly problematic when computing derived metrics such as closure speed, where non-monotonic noise propagates into erroneous rate estimates.

**Training data requirements.** Machine-learning approaches, including U-Net-based architectures (Ronneberger et al., 2015; Dogru et al., 2024; Naser et al., 2025) and foundation models such as the Segment Anything Model (Lehmann et al., 2024), require annotated ground-truth masks. These annotations are themselves subject to observer bias and are expensive to produce at scale. Deep-learning pipelines further introduce domain-shift risks when applied to imaging conditions different from their training distribution (Oldenburg et al., 2021).

**Lack of biological constraints.** No existing tool enforces the physical reality that a properly conducted scratch wound can only close over time. While post-hoc smoothing can reduce noise, it does not encode the underlying biological axiom and may introduce artifacts of its own.

### 1.3 Biological Context: Myoblast Migration Under Pharmacological Modulation

Cell migration and proliferation are fundamental processes governing tissue repair, development, and pathological states including fibrosis and metastatic dissemination (Haeger et al., 2013; Ele et al., 2020). In skeletal muscle, satellite cell activation, migration, and differentiation are critical for regeneration following injury (Goetsch et al., 2013; Webster and Fan, 2013; Kowalski et al., 2017). The scratch-wound assay provides a reproducible *in vitro* model for studying these processes in myoblasts (Goetsch et al., 2011).

Two pharmacological targets are of particular interest in the context of skeletal muscle repair:

**The renin-angiotensin system (RAS).** Angiotensin II (AngII), acting through the AT1 receptor (AT1R), exerts pleiotropic effects on skeletal muscle biology, including inhibition of satellite cell proliferation (Yoshida et al., 2013), regulation of satellite cell chemotaxis (Yoshida et al., 2010), and promotion of myofibroblast transdifferentiation (Lemos et al., 2019). AT1R blockade with losartan was shown to attenuate TGF-$\beta$-induced failure of muscle regeneration in multiple myopathic states (Cohn et al., 2007) and to improve muscle regeneration in non-dystrophic injury models (Bedair et al., 2008). These findings position AT1R blockers (ARBs) as potential modulators of myoblast migration, though the effects are context-dependent: AT1R deficiency in skin wound models delays healing and reduces angiogenesis (Kurosaka et al., 2009). Candesartan, a selective AT1R antagonist, was selected for this study as a clinically available ARB with well-characterized pharmacology (Cabello-Verrugio et al., 2015).

**The TGF-$\beta$/ALK5 pathway.** Transforming growth factor-$\beta$ (TGF-$\beta$) signaling through ALK5 (TGF-$\beta$ receptor type I) plays a dual role in skeletal muscle: it drives myogenic-to-fibrotic cell fate switching (Li et al., 2004; Loiselle et al., 2009), suppresses myoblast differentiation via Smad3-mediated repression of myogenic transcription factors (McFarland et al., 2001), and curbs cell fusion during regeneration (Girardi et al., 2021). Pharmacological ALK5 inhibition has been shown to attenuate muscle fibrosis in dystrophic models (Ceco and McNally, 2013; Cohn et al., 2007) and cancer cachexia (Lach-Trifilieff et al., 2019), and recent work has systematically compared TGF-$\beta$ receptor I inhibitors for their effects on myotube formation (Weiss et al., 2025a) and myofibroblast differentiation (Weiss et al., 2025b). Notably, TGF-$\beta$ also influences collective cell migration by modulating leader cell positioning at the wound edge (Vishwakarma et al., 2014), suggesting that ALK5 inhibition could directly impact wound closure dynamics.

Critically, AngII and TGF-$\beta$ signaling pathways converge at the Smad2/3 level: AngII activates Smad3 independently of TGF-$\beta$ ligand (Rodriguez-Vita et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2008), and both pathways synergize to promote fibrosis (Murphy et al., 2015). This crosstalk provides the rationale for testing both AT1R blockade and ALK5 inhibition in the same experimental framework.

### 1.4 Our Contribution

We introduce **Quantification**, a two-stage pipeline that addresses the limitations of existing wound quantification tools:

1. A **variance-based single-frame detector** that exploits the fundamental texture difference between cell monolayer (high local variance due to cell edges, organelles, and phase-contrast halos) and the wound channel (smooth, low variance). This approach is grounded in the physics of phase-contrast image formation (Yin et al., 2012) and follows the precedent of training-free texture-based segmentation in label-free microscopy (Ambrossio et al., 2022; Vicar et al., 2019). No training data or threshold tuning by the user is required.

2. A **stateful temporal integrator** that encodes the monotonic closure axiom directly into the segmentation: once a pixel column has been declared healed, it cannot revert. This eliminates non-biological noise in the area-versus-time curve and produces smooth, strictly non-increasing wound trajectories. While temporal constraints have been explored in cell tracking (Bragantini et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2023; Li et al., 2008) and video segmentation (Guan et al., 2021), to our knowledge this is the first application of a hard monotonic constraint to wound-healing segmentation.

The pipeline is complemented by automated multi-level quality control, parallel trajectory processing, and downstream metric extraction (closure speed, edge displacement, closing fraction). We validate Quantification on a pharmacological study of wound closure in immortalized myoblasts treated with candesartan and an ALK5 inhibitor, demonstrating 98.9% detection accuracy against expert annotation and biologically interpretable results across 282 trajectories.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Cell Culture and Experimental Design

#### 2.1.1 Cell Lines

Two immortalized human skeletal muscle cell lines (myoblasts) were employed:

- **iMC ISOR544C**: Derived from normal muscle tissue (isogenic control)
- **iMC MUTR544C**: Derived from dystrophic muscle tissue (carrying the MUTR544 mutation)

This design enabled assessment of whether drug responsiveness differs between normal and dystrophic myoblasts, yielding insights into cell-autonomous versus context-dependent pharmacological effects. Cells were maintained under standard culture conditions and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

#### 2.1.2 Experimental Design

A full factorial design was employed:

| Factor | Levels | Description |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Cell line | 2 | iMC ISOR544C, iMC MUTR544C |
| Treatment | 4 | DMSO (vehicle), Candesartan, ALK5 inhibitor, Media (basal) |
| Concentration | 4 | 0.0, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0 mM |
| Experiment | 2 | EXP1, EXP2 (independent biological replicates) |

This design yielded 2 $\times$ 4 $\times$ 4 $\times$ 2 = 64 treatment conditions. Two independent experiments served as biological replicates: EXP1 ($n$ = 158 wells) and EXP2 ($n$ = 167 wells), yielding 325 total wound regions.

#### 2.1.3 Pharmacological Agents

**Candesartan** is a selective antagonist of the angiotensin II type-1 receptor (AT1R). Based on evidence that AT1R signaling inhibits satellite cell proliferation (Yoshida et al., 2013) and chemotaxis (Yoshida et al., 2010), and that AT1R blockade improves muscle regeneration (Cohn et al., 2007; Bedair et al., 2008), we hypothesized that candesartan would modulate scratch-wound closure in myoblasts.

**ALK5 inhibitor** is a selective antagonist of the TGF-$\beta$ receptor type I (ALK5). Given that TGF-$\beta$ signaling promotes myogenic-to-fibrotic transdifferentiation (Li et al., 2004), suppresses myoblast differentiation (McFarland et al., 2001), and modulates leader cell positioning during collective migration (Vishwakarma et al., 2014), we hypothesized that ALK5 inhibition would suppress wound closure kinetics.

Concentrations of 0.01, 0.1, and 1.0 mM were selected to assess dose-response relationships across a pharmacologically relevant range. DMSO served as vehicle control; media without supplementation provided a basal reference.

### 2.2 Scratch-Wound Creation and Time-Lapse Microscopy

Confluent monolayers were wounded by standardized mechanical scratch. Treatment medium was applied immediately after wounding. Time-lapse imaging commenced at $t = 0$ (baseline, fully open wound) and continued for approximately 24 hours with images acquired at $\sim$1-hour intervals, yielding 20--25 timepoints per trajectory. Images were acquired at approximately 900 $\times$ 735 pixels per field of view.

### 2.3 Automated Wound Segmentation: The Quantification Pipeline

The Quantification pipeline operates in two stages: (1) a single-frame detector that extracts wound boundaries from each image independently, and (2) a temporal integrator that enforces the monotonic closure constraint across the time series. We describe each stage in detail below.

#### 2.3.1 Problem Formulation

Let $I(x, y, t) \in [0, 1]$ denote the intensity-normalized grayscale image at spatial position $(x, y)$ and discrete timepoint $t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, T\}$. The image has dimensions $H \times W$ (rows $\times$ columns). We seek a binary wound mask:

$$M(x, y, t) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if pixel } (x, y) \text{ is wound at time } t \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

and per-column edge functions:

$$u(x, t) = \min\{y : M(x, y, t) = 1\} \quad \text{(upper edge)}$$
$$\ell(x, t) = \max\{y : M(x, y, t) = 1\} \quad \text{(lower edge)}$$

The total wound area at time $t$ is:

$$A(t) = \sum_{x=0}^{W-1} \max\bigl(0,\; \ell(x,t) - u(x,t) + 1\bigr)$$

**Biological axiom (monotonic closure).** In a properly conducted scratch-wound assay, wound area is a monotonically non-increasing function of time:

$$\boxed{A(t+1) \leq A(t) \quad \forall\; t \geq 0}$$

We enforce this constraint at the per-column edge level (Section 2.3.3), which is strictly stronger than the aggregate area constraint.

#### 2.3.2 Single-Frame Detection

The single-frame detector implements a seven-stage pipeline that transforms a raw grayscale image into a validated wound mask with smooth boundary curves.

**Stage 1: Local Variance Texture Map.** Cell monolayers exhibit high spatial variance due to cell borders, nuclei, and phase-contrast halos, while the wound channel is comparatively smooth (Yin et al., 2012). We use local variance as a texture discriminator, following the precedent of variance-based segmentation in biomedical imaging (Koenig et al., 2017; Loizou et al., 2016; Ambrossio et al., 2022). For a square window of side length $w$ (default $w = 15$), the local variance at each pixel is computed via the uniform-filter identity:

$$\sigma^2(x, y) = \langle I^2 \rangle_w(x, y) - \bigl[\langle I \rangle_w(x, y)\bigr]^2$$

where $\langle \cdot \rangle_w$ denotes the box-filter mean over a $w \times w$ neighborhood. Both terms are computed using `scipy.ndimage.uniform_filter`, yielding $O(HW)$ complexity independent of window size (the filter is separable and computed via running sums). The raw variance map is post-smoothed with a separable Gaussian kernel ($\sigma_\text{smooth} = 2.0$ pixels).

**Stage 2: Y-Band Detection.** The wound is a horizontal band spanning nearly the full image width. We identify the vertical range $[y_\text{min}, y_\text{max}]$ by computing the row-averaged variance profile $\bar{V}(y)$, smoothing with a 1-D Gaussian kernel ($\sigma_y = 10.0$ pixels), locating the global minimum $y_c$, and extracting the contiguous segment of sub-threshold rows (35th percentile) containing $y_c$, expanded by a margin fraction of 0.15.

**Stage 3: Column-Wise Edge Extraction.** Within the Y-band, upper and lower wound boundaries are extracted for each column by searching for the variance transition between cell (high $\sigma^2$) and wound (low $\sigma^2$) regions. The edge-detection threshold $\tau$ is derived adaptively from the cell regions flanking the wound: $\tau = 0.5 \cdot \text{median}(\sigma^2_\text{cell}})$. This adaptive threshold adjusts to the actual cell texture intensity of each image. Edge search is fully vectorized using `np.argmax` on boolean arrays.

**Stage 4: RANSAC Outlier Rejection.** Raw edges may contain outliers caused by imaging artifacts, debris, or partial cell detachment. We apply an iterative polynomial-fit-and-reject scheme inspired by RANSAC (Fischler and Bolles, 1981). A degree-2 polynomial (parabolic) captures the gentle curvature typical of scratch wounds while rejecting localized spikes. Three iterations with a maximum deviation threshold of 25 pixels suffice for convergence in practice.

**Stage 5: Savitzky--Golay Smoothing.** Residual high-frequency jitter is removed with a Savitzky--Golay filter (Savitzky and Golay, 1964), which preserves edge shape without phase distortion. Parameters: window length $w_\text{sg} = 51$, polynomial order $p_\text{sg} = 3$. Remaining NaN values are filled by linear interpolation prior to filtering.

**Stage 6: Mask Reconstruction.** The binary mask is reconstructed from smooth edges using vectorized broadcasting:

$$M(x, y) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[u(x) \leq y \leq \ell(x)\bigr]$$

This is implemented as a single NumPy operation at $O(HW)$ complexity with no Python loops.

**Stage 7: Geometric Validation.** Four quality-control criteria are evaluated on each mask:

| Criterion | Threshold | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Width coverage $r_w$ | $> 0.75$ | Wound must span most of image width |
| Aspect ratio $\rho$ | $> 1.5$ | Wound is wider than tall |
| Y-position $\bar{y}_\text{frac}$ | $\in (0.15, 0.85)$ | Wound is roughly centered |
| Thickness CV | $< 0.60$ | Consistent thickness across columns |

A detection passes QC if and only if all four criteria are satisfied. When the primary pipeline fails, an optional fallback strategy is activated using constrained region growing from a seed at $y_c$ with morphological cleanup (hole filling at 500 px, small-object removal at 1000 px, disk closing at radius 5).

#### 2.3.3 Temporal Constraint: Monotonic Closure

The Quantification temporal integrator wraps the single-frame detector with a stateful constraint that enforces the biological axiom at the per-column edge level. The pipeline implements two complementary mechanisms: (1) a Kalman-filtered monotonic constraint (default, `use_kalman=True`) that attenuates spurious detector outputs while respecting the biological invariant; and (2) a hard safety net that guarantees the invariant unconditionally regardless of filter output. Four numerical safeguards prevent failure-mode propagation across frames.

**Notation.** Let $u_\text{det}(x, t)$ and $\ell_\text{det}(x, t)$ denote the raw single-frame detector output (Section 2.3.2), $u(x, t)$ and $\ell(x, t)$ the constrained edges, and $P(x, t)$ the per-column Kalman uncertainty.

**Initialization** ($t = 0$):
$$u(x, 0) = u_\text{det}(x, 0), \qquad \ell(x, 0) = \ell_\text{det}(x, 0)$$

The $t = 0$ edges are accepted without constraint and serve as the global reference. The Kalman state is initialised to these values with initial uncertainty $P(x, 0) = \max(R_\text{base},\; 1.0)$ px².

**Kalman-filtered constraint** ($t > 0$, primary mechanism):

We model each column's wound edge as an independent scalar state. The update at each frame proceeds in three sub-steps.

*Prediction.* The predicted state is the previous constrained edge; predicted uncertainty grows by process noise $Q$:
$$\hat{u}^-(x, t) = u(x, t-1), \qquad P^-(x, t) = P(x, t-1) + Q$$

Process noise $Q$ (px²) quantifies expected frame-to-frame edge movement variance. By default, $Q$ is estimated empirically from the set of QC-validated trajectories $\mathcal{C}$ as the variance of consecutive frame-to-frame edge differences:
$$\hat{Q} = \operatorname{Var}\!\Bigl(\bigl\{\,u_\text{det}(x,t{+}1) - u_\text{det}(x,t)\;\big|\; x,\,t \in \mathcal{C}\bigr\}\Bigr)$$

A conservative default $Q_0 = 400\;\text{px}^2$, derived from the expected cell migration rate of 10–30 µm h⁻¹ at 1.24 µm px⁻¹ and 0.5 h acquisition intervals, is used on first run before cached trajectories exist.

*Spatial pre-smoothing.* The raw detected edge is smoothed with a 1-D Gaussian kernel ($\sigma_\text{spatial} = 5$ columns) before the Kalman update to suppress column-level artefacts such as debris or bubble shadows that would otherwise be locked in by the subsequent monotonic constraint:
$$\tilde{u}_\text{det}(x, t) = \bigl(G_{\sigma} * u_\text{det}(\cdot,\, t)\bigr)(x)$$

*Per-column observation noise.* Observation noise $R(x, t)$ is estimated from three data-driven signals that capture distinct sources of detector unreliability:
$$R(x, t) = R_\text{base}(t) \cdot C^{-2}(x, t) \cdot J(x, t) \cdot m_\text{method}$$

where:

| Term | Definition | Rationale |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| $R_\text{base}(t)$ | $\operatorname{Var}_{x}\!\bigl(u_\text{det}(x,t) - \tilde{u}_\text{det}(x,t)\bigr)$ | Frame-level detector noise from edge residuals |
| $C(x,t)$ | $\bigl(\sigma^2_\text{cell}(x,t) - \sigma^2_\text{wound}(x,t)\bigr)/\sigma^2_\text{cell}(x,t)$, clipped to $[0.01, 1.0]$ | Local wound-vs-cell variance contrast; low contrast → high $R$ |
| $J(x,t)$ | $1 + \bigl(|u_\text{det}(x,t) - u(x,t{-}1)| \;/\; \widetilde{\text{med}}_x\bigr)^2$ | Jump penalty; large deviations from prediction → high $R$ |
| $m_\text{method}$ | 3.0 (fallback detector), 1.0 (primary) | Penalises less reliable fallback detections |

The median-normalised jump $\widetilde{\text{med}}_x = \max\!\bigl(\operatorname{median}_x |u_\text{det} - u_\text{prev}|,\; 1.0\bigr)$ is computed per frame.

*Kalman update.* The gain $K(x,t) \in [0, 1]$ blends prediction and observation:
$$K(x, t) = \frac{P^-(x, t)}{P^-(x, t) + R(x, t)}$$

$$u_\text{filt}(x, t) = u(x, t-1) + K(x, t)\bigl[\tilde{u}_\text{det}(x, t) - u(x, t-1)\bigr]$$

$$P_\text{upd}(x, t) = \bigl(1 - K(x, t)\bigr) \cdot P^-(x, t)$$

High $R$ (noisy or fallback observation) → small $K$ → filter relies on the monotonic prior. Low $R$ (clean, high-contrast frame) → large $K$ → filter trusts the detector.

**Hard safety net** (secondary guarantee):

After the Kalman blend, the biological invariant is enforced unconditionally at the per-column level:
$$\boxed{u(x, t) = \max\bigl(u_\text{filt}(x, t),\; u(x, t-1)\bigr)}$$
$$\boxed{\ell(x, t) = \min\bigl(\ell_\text{filt}(x, t),\; \ell(x, t-1)\bigr)}$$

This guarantees $A(t) \leq A(t-1)$ for all $t > 0$ regardless of the Kalman output. When constrained edges meet or cross at column $x$ ($u(x,t) \geq \ell(x,t)$), that column contributes zero wound width to $A(t)$. No artificial minimum gap is imposed, eliminating systematic area overestimation at late timepoints.

**Wound closure freeze.** When the previous-frame wound area falls below 1% of the $t=0$ reference ($\text{prev\_area} / A(0) < \theta_\text{close} = 0.01$), the wound is declared fully closed and all subsequent frames inherit the preceding frame's edges. This prevents phantom detections in biologically closed wounds.

**Numerical safeguards.**

Four safeguards prevent numerical failure from propagating across frames:

1. *R NaN guard.* If column-wise jump values $|u_\text{det}(x,t) - u(x,t{-}1)|$ are non-finite — arising from NaN edge estimates in prior frames or degenerate variance maps — they are replaced with zero before computing $J(x,t)$ (treating the jump as neutral, with no penalty). Any residual non-finite $R$ values are then replaced with $Q_0 = 400$ px² before the Kalman update, ensuring $K$ is always a well-defined scalar in $[0, 1]$.

2. *Kalman output NaN guard.* After the update step, any column where $u_\text{filt}(x,t)$ is non-finite is reset to $u(x,t{-}1)$ before the hard safety net is applied. This breaks the NaN propagation chain: a column whose state is corrupted at frame $t$ cannot corrupt frames $t{+}1, t{+}2, \ldots$

3. *P-state correction after safety-net activation.* When the hard safety net overrides the Kalman estimate for column $x$ (i.e., $u(x,t) \neq u_\text{filt}(x,t)$), the stored uncertainty is reset to the predicted value $P^-(x,t)$ rather than the contracted $P_\text{upd}(x,t)$:
$$P(x, t) = \begin{cases} P^-(x, t) & \text{if } u(x,t) \neq u_\text{filt}(x,t) \\ P_\text{upd}(x, t) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
Rationale: $P_\text{upd}$ quantifies uncertainty in $u_\text{filt}$, not in the constrained value $u(x,t{-}1)$. Retaining the contracted $P_\text{upd}$ after a safety-net correction would make the filter systematically overconfident at those columns on subsequent frames, suppressing $K$ and causing it to under-weight genuine late-stage closure signals. Resetting to $P^-$ correctly conveys that the constraint provided no new information about the true edge position.

4. *Last-resort monotonic backstop.* After all constraint paths, the total area $A(t)$ is compared to $A(t{-}1)$. If $A(t) > A(t{-}1)$ — which should not occur given safeguards 1–3 — the frame is silently reverted to the previous edges and a `RuntimeWarning` is raised identifying the trajectory identifier, timepoint, and area values. This backstop makes upstream regressions immediately detectable in production runs without silently corrupting the dataset.

**Complexity.** The Kalman update (per-column gain, blend, uncertainty update) adds $O(W)$ operations per frame, dominated by the per-column variance contrast computation at $O(HW)$. Total per-frame complexity remains $O(HW)$.

#### 2.3.4 Trajectory Processing and Parallel Execution

For each biological sample (identified by a unique sample $\times$ experiment $\times$ exposure tuple), images are sorted by acquisition time, fed sequentially to the Quantification segmenter, and results serialized to pickle and CSV formats. Independent samples are processed in parallel using Python's `concurrent.futures.ProcessPoolExecutor` with explicitly controlled worker count ($n_\text{workers}$, default 4, capped at available CPU cores). Each worker instantiates its own segmenter with independent state, ensuring no shared mutable data.

### 2.4 Quality Control

Quality control operates at three hierarchical levels:

**Level 1: Single-frame geometric validation** (Section 2.3.2, Stage 7). Applied automatically to every frame; flags implausible geometry.

**Level 2: Baseline distributional analysis.** The $t = 0$ mask undergoes additional scrutiny. The row-wise projection $P_y(y) = \sum_x M(x, y, 0)$, smoothed with a Gaussian kernel ($\sigma = 15$), must exhibit exactly one prominent peak with center within $\pm 15\%$ of the mask centroid. The column-wise projection $P_x(x) = \sum_y M(x, y, 0)$, normalized to $[0, 1]$, must satisfy $|\text{skew}(P_x)| < 0.8$ and central-band flatness $\text{std}(P_x[0.25W : 0.75W]) < 0.15$.

**Level 3: Manual expert curation.** For high-stakes analyses, baseline images are reviewed by a human expert using an interactive web-based annotation tool. Each segmentation is classified as correct, incorrect, or no-wound. For a subset of images, the reviewer draws ground-truth polygon annotations enabling pixel-level validation.

### 2.5 Downstream Metrics

#### 2.5.1 Fractional Wound Closure

Raw wound area measurements were corrected using a monotonic non-increasing constraint with piecewise cubic Hermite interpolation (PCHIP):

$$A_{\text{corrected}}(t) = \min\bigl(A(t),\; A_{\text{corrected}}(t-1)\bigr)$$

Fractional wound closure was defined as:

$$f(t) = \frac{A(0) - A_{\text{corrected}}(t)}{A(0)}$$

ranging from 0 (no closure) to 1 (complete closure).

#### 2.5.2 Time Grid Alignment

Per-trajectory measurements, acquired at irregular intervals (nominally 1 hour), were aligned to a common grid with 0.5-hour resolution using linear interpolation (0--1440 minutes, 49 aligned timepoints).

#### 2.5.3 Edge Displacement and Closure Speed

Per-edge displacements between $t = 0$ and target time $t_T$:

$$\Delta u(x) = u(x, t_T) - u(x, 0), \qquad \Delta \ell(x) = \ell(x, 0) - \ell(x, t_T)$$

Mean closure speed:

$$v_\text{mean} = \frac{v_\text{upper} + v_\text{lower}}{2}, \quad v_\text{edge} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{V}} \frac{|\Delta_\text{edge}(x)|}{t_T}$$

#### 2.5.4 Wound Closing Determination

A trajectory is classified as "closing" if $\geq 60\%$ of valid columns show inward edge motion at both boundaries.

### 2.6 Statistical Analysis

Population-level statistics were computed per condition at each aligned timepoint:

$$\bar{f}(t) = \frac{1}{N_\text{condition}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_\text{condition}} f_i(t), \qquad \text{SEM}(t) = \frac{\text{SD}(t)}{\sqrt{N_\text{condition}}}$$

Fractional closure was reported at 12 and 24 hours. Time to 50% closure was estimated via linear interpolation between bracketing timepoints. Concentration-response relationships were analyzed by comparing fractional closure at 24 hours across drug concentrations within each treatment group. Cell line comparisons and inter-experiment reproducibility were assessed by stratifying results by cell line and experiment, respectively.

All per-column operations are implemented using NumPy broadcasting and fancy indexing. Edge extraction uses `np.argmax` on 2-D boolean arrays; mask reconstruction uses rank-broadcasting; the monotonic constraint uses element-wise `np.maximum`/`np.minimum`. The pipeline is fully deterministic and reproducible.

**Computational complexity per frame:**

| Stage | Complexity | Notes |
|-------|------------|-------|
| Local variance | $O(HW)$ | Separable uniform filter |
| Y-band detection | $O(H + W)$ | 1-D profile |
| Edge extraction | $O(HW)$ | Vectorized boolean argmax |
| RANSAC | $O(Wk)$ | $k = 3$ iterations |
| Savitzky--Golay | $O(W)$ | Single pass |
| Mask reconstruction | $O(HW)$ | Broadcasting |
| Geometric validation | $O(HW)$ | Summary statistics |
| Spatial edge smoothing (Kalman pre-step) | $O(W)$ | `gaussian_filter1d`, $\sigma = 5$ |
| Per-column R estimation | $O(HW)$ | Variance contrast requires full variance map |
| Kalman predict–update | $O(W)$ | Vectorized gain, blend, uncertainty |
| Hard safety net | $O(W)$ | Element-wise `maximum`/`minimum` |
| P-state correction | $O(W)$ | Element-wise conditional assignment |
| Monotonic backstop check | $O(1)$ | Scalar area comparison |

Total per frame: $O(HW)$, dominated by the variance map computation. For a typical $1024 \times 1280$ image this corresponds to $\sim 1.3 \times 10^6$ operations per stage.

**Numerical safeguards.** Four safeguards guard against failure-mode propagation across frames (see Section 2.3.3 for formal statement): (i) non-finite observation noise $R$ is clamped to the conservative default $Q_0 = 400$ px²; (ii) non-finite Kalman filter outputs are reset to the previous constrained edge before the safety net; (iii) per-column uncertainty $P$ is reset to the predicted value $P^-$ when the safety net overrides the Kalman estimate, preventing overconfidence propagation; and (iv) a last-resort area comparison after all constraint paths emits a `RuntimeWarning` and reverts to the previous frame if $A(t) > A(t{-}1)$ persists. All four operations are $O(W)$ and do not affect the asymptotic complexity of the pipeline.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Methodological Advances

The Quantification pipeline offers several advances over existing scratch-wound quantification tools:

**Training-free segmentation.** Unlike deep-learning approaches that require annotated datasets (Ronneberger et al., 2015; Dogru et al., 2024; Lehmann et al., 2024; Naser et al., 2025) and suffer from domain shift when applied to novel imaging conditions (Oldenburg et al., 2021), Quantification exploits a fundamental physical property of phase-contrast image formation: cells scatter light and produce high local variance, while the empty wound channel does not (Yin et al., 2012). This approach aligns with growing evidence that classical texture-based methods can compete with machine learning on small-to-medium datasets in label-free microscopy (Ambrossio et al., 2022; Vicar et al., 2019). The 98.9% detection accuracy achieved without any training data is competitive with or exceeds reported performance of supervised methods on comparable assays.

**Biologically grounded temporal constraint.** The monotonic closure axiom is not merely a regularizer --- it encodes a physical law of the assay system. While temporal constraints have been explored in cell tracking (Bragantini et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2023) and video segmentation (Guan et al., 2021), prior wound-healing tools process frames independently. The per-column monotonic constraint is strictly stronger than a global area constraint: it enforces spatial coherence at every column, preventing the non-biological area fluctuations that plague single-frame methods and that Treloar and Simpson (2013) identified as a major source of quantification error.

**Robust edge detection.** The combination of RANSAC-inspired outlier rejection (Fischler and Bolles, 1981) with Savitzky--Golay smoothing (Savitzky and Golay, 1964) provides a principled two-stage approach to boundary regularization. The polynomial fit captures genuine wound curvature while rejecting localized artifacts, and the filter preserves edge shape without phase distortion --- properties well-suited to the geometry of scratch wounds.

**Computational efficiency.** Full vectorization ensures $O(HW)$ complexity per frame, dominated by array operations rather than Python overhead. A typical 20-frame trajectory processes in under 10 seconds on commodity hardware, enabling real-time analysis of high-content screening experiments. This efficiency is particularly relevant as high-throughput wound-healing platforms scale to 96- and 384-well formats (Zordan et al., 2011; Yarrow et al., 2004; Schmidt et al., 2024).

**Multi-level quality control.** The three-tier QC system --- geometric validation, distributional analysis, and optional manual curation --- provides hierarchical confidence in segmentation quality. The perfect sensitivity (1.00) for wound-bearing images ensures that no valid segmentation is erroneously rejected, while the moderate specificity for no-wound images (0.56) represents a known limitation that can be addressed by supplementing the variance-based QC with intensity-profile features.

### 4.2 Biological Findings

The wound healing dynamics observed in this study are consistent with the known biology of the pharmacological targets.

**Candesartan showed no significant effect on wound closure.** Despite evidence that AngII regulates satellite cell chemotaxis (Yoshida et al., 2010) and proliferation (Yoshida et al., 2013), AT1R blockade with candesartan did not substantially alter myoblast wound closure kinetics relative to DMSO vehicle control (20.0% vs. 19.7% at 24 h). This may reflect cell-type-specific differences in AT1R expression or signaling, or the possibility that the pro-migratory and anti-migratory effects of AngII signaling are balanced in this myoblast system. Notably, AT1R deficiency delays wound healing in skin models (Kurosaka et al., 2009), suggesting that the net effect of AT1R blockade on cell migration is context-dependent and may differ between tissue types.

**ALK5 inhibition reduced wound closure.** The ALK5i-treated group showed the lowest fractional closure (15.5% at 24 h), consistent with the role of TGF-$\beta$ in promoting collective cell migration through leader cell positioning (Vishwakarma et al., 2014) and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition-related motility pathways. While TGF-$\beta$ is often considered anti-regenerative in skeletal muscle due to its pro-fibrotic effects (Li et al., 2004; Ceco and McNally, 2013; Delaney et al., 2017), the reduced wound closure observed here suggests that ALK5 signaling also contributes to the migratory component of wound repair in myoblasts. This finding aligns with recent work showing that TGF-$\beta$ receptor I inhibitors have complex, dose-dependent effects on myoblast biology (Weiss et al., 2025a; Weiss et al., 2025b).

**No condition reached 50% closure.** The observation that all conditions achieved less than 20% closure in 24 hours, with none reaching 50%, is notable. This may reflect the inherently slower migratory phenotype of skeletal muscle myoblasts compared to epithelial cell lines commonly used in wound-healing assays (Goetsch et al., 2011; Goetsch et al., 2013), or the specific culture conditions employed. The slow closure kinetics suggest that longer observation windows (48--72 h) may be necessary to fully characterize dose-response relationships in myoblast systems, as has been noted for other slowly migrating cell types (Topman et al., 2021).

**AngII--TGF-$\beta$ crosstalk.** The differential effects of candesartan (no change) and ALK5 inhibitor (reduced closure) are informative in light of the known convergence of AngII and TGF-$\beta$ signaling at the Smad2/3 level (Rodriguez-Vita et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2008; Murphy et al., 2015). The divergent outcomes suggest that, in this myoblast system, the migratory-relevant TGF-$\beta$ signaling operates predominantly through the canonical ALK5-Smad pathway rather than through AngII-mediated Smad activation. This has implications for the design of combination therapies targeting muscle fibrosis and regeneration.

### 4.4 Limitations

Several known limitations warrant discussion:

**Irreversible per-column constraint.** If the detector produces a spurious inward edge at time $t$ (due to debris, bubble, or focus artifact), the hard monotonic constraint permanently propagates that error to all subsequent frames for the affected columns. This limitation has been mitigated by the introduction of a Kalman-filtered constraint (default behaviour), which assigns high observation noise to spurious observations, causing the filter to rely on the monotonic prediction instead. The hard safety net preserves the biological invariant while preventing permanent error lock-in. The original hard constraint remains available via `use_kalman=False` for reproducibility.

**Fixed threshold heuristic.** The edge-detection threshold ($\tau = 0.5 \cdot \text{median}(\sigma^2_\text{cell})$) assumes unimodal cell variance and a clean cell-wound boundary. Images with heterogeneous cell populations or illumination gradients may require adaptive thresholding.

**Integer-pixel edges.** Edges are extracted at integer-pixel resolution. For thin wounds approaching closure, the 1-pixel quantization error represents a significant fraction of remaining width. Sub-pixel edge localization via parabolic interpolation would improve precision.

**No uncertainty quantification.** The pipeline produces point estimates without confidence intervals. Bootstrapping area estimates under small edge perturbations would provide uncertainty bounds, which are essential for rigorous statistical inference.

**No physical calibration.** All measurements are in pixel units. Incorporating pixel-to-micrometer conversion from microscope metadata would yield physically meaningful areas and speeds.

**Limited ground-truth annotations.** Pixel-level validation was performed on only 5 images. A larger annotated dataset would provide tighter estimates of segmentation accuracy and enable per-condition stratification of performance.

**Slow myoblast closure.** The modest closure observed ($<$20% in 24 h) limits the statistical power of dose-response comparisons. The narrow dynamic range may mask true pharmacological effects that would become apparent over longer observation windows.

### 4.5 Future Directions

Planned improvements include: (1) adaptive per-column thresholding using local statistics or Otsu's method; (2) sub-pixel edge extraction via parabolic fitting of the variance profile around the threshold crossing; (3) ~~a Kalman-filter framework combining detector output with monotonic prediction~~ (implemented; see `kalman_constraint.py`); (4) bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification for area measurements; (5) integration of pixel-size metadata for calibrated physical measurements; and (6) extension to non-horizontal wound geometries via principal-axis rotation.

From a biological perspective, mathematical models such as the Fisher-Kolmogorov reaction-diffusion framework (Jin et al., 2015; Maini et al., 2020; Simpson et al., 2013) could be applied to the Quantification-derived wound area trajectories to disentangle the contributions of cell migration and proliferation to wound closure, providing mechanistic insight beyond the aggregate closure rate.

## 5. Conclusion

We have presented Quantification, a fully automated pipeline for scratch-wound healing quantification that combines variance-based single-frame detection with a biologically motivated monotonic closure constraint. The pipeline requires no training data, processes standard phase-contrast microscopy images in linear time, and produces smooth, strictly non-increasing wound area trajectories. Validation on 325 images from a pharmacological study in immortalized myoblasts demonstrated 98.9% detection accuracy and a mean Dice coefficient of 0.91 against expert annotations. Application to 282 validated trajectories revealed that ALK5 inhibition suppressed wound closure kinetics while candesartan had no appreciable effect, consistent with a dominant role for canonical TGF-$\beta$ signaling in myoblast migration. A three-tier quality-control system ensures robust performance, and parallel trajectory processing enables scalable analysis of high-content experiments. The mathematical formulation is presented in full, all parameters are documented, and the implementation is released as open-source Python code. We anticipate that Quantification will reduce the time and subjectivity currently associated with scratch-wound quantification, enabling researchers to focus on biological interpretation rather than manual measurement.
