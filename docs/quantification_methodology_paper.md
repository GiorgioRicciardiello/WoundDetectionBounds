## Abstract

Scratch-wound healing assays are a cornerstone of *in vitro* cell migration studies, yet quantification remains largely manual and prone to inter-observer variability. We present **Quantification**, a fully automated computational pipeline that segments wound boundaries from phase-contrast time-lapse microscopy and enforces a biologically motivated monotonic closure constraint across the temporal sequence. The pipeline operates in two stages: (1) a single-frame detector that extracts wound boundaries via local variance analysis, RANSAC-based outlier rejection, and Savitzky--Golay smoothing, followed by (2) a stateful temporal integrator that constrains per-column edge positions to move only inward over time, encoding the axiom that scratch wounds can only heal. The system requires no machine-learning training data, operates on standard brightfield or phase-contrast images, and processes a typical 20-frame trajectory in under 10 seconds on commodity hardware. We describe the mathematical formulation, algorithmic design, quality-control criteria, and downstream metric extraction. A multi-tier validation comprising geometric plausibility checks, distributional analysis of baseline masks, and optional manual curation ensures robust performance across diverse experimental conditions. The pipeline is implemented in Python 3.11 with fully vectorized NumPy operations and supports parallel processing of independent biological samples. We provide the complete open-source implementation together with configuration defaults tuned for 10x-magnification scratch-wound images of approximately 1024 $\times$ 1280 pixels.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Scratch-Wound Assay

The scratch-wound (or wound-healing) assay is one of the most widely used *in vitro* methods for studying collective cell migration (Liang et al., 2007; Jonkman et al., 2014). A confluent cell monolayer is mechanically disrupted---typically by drawing a pipette tip across the surface---creating a cell-free gap. Time-lapse phase-contrast microscopy then records the progressive closure of this gap as cells at the wound margin proliferate and migrate inward. The rate and completeness of wound closure serve as proxies for migratory capacity and are routinely used to evaluate drug effects, gene knockdowns, and phenotypic differences between cell lines.

### 1.2 Limitations of Current Approaches

Despite its conceptual simplicity, quantifying wound healing remains a bottleneck. Manual measurement of wound area from exported images introduces inter-observer variability, is labor-intensive (especially for multi-well, multi-timepoint experiments), and does not scale to the hundreds of trajectories generated by modern high-content screening platforms. Several computational tools have been proposed---including TScratch (Gebäck et al., 2009), ImageJ macros (Suarez-Arnedo et al., 2020), and deep-learning segmenters (Vu et al., 2023)---but they share common limitations:

- **Single-frame independence.** Most tools process each image in isolation, ignoring the temporal structure of the experiment and producing noisy area-versus-time curves with non-biological fluctuations (apparent wound re-opening).
- **Training data requirements.** Machine-learning approaches require annotated ground truth masks, which themselves suffer from observer bias.
- **Lack of biological constraints.** No existing tool enforces the physical reality that a properly conducted scratch wound can only close over time.

### 1.3 Our Contribution

We introduce **Quantification**, a two-stage pipeline that addresses these limitations:

1. A **variance-based single-frame detector** that exploits the fundamental texture difference between cell monolayer (high local variance due to cell edges, organelles, and phase-contrast halos) and the wound channel (smooth, low variance). No training data or threshold tuning by the user is required.

2. A **stateful temporal integrator** that encodes the monotonic closure axiom directly into the segmentation: once a pixel column has been declared healed, it cannot revert. This eliminates non-biological noise in the area-versus-time curve and produces smooth, strictly non-increasing wound trajectories.

The pipeline is complemented by automated quality control, parallel trajectory processing, and downstream metric extraction (closure speed, edge displacement, closing fraction), providing a complete end-to-end solution from raw microscopy images to publication-ready quantitative data.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Problem Formulation

#### Notation

Let $I(x, y, t) \in [0, 1]$ denote the intensity-normalized grayscale image at spatial position $(x, y)$ and discrete timepoint $t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, T\}$. The image has dimensions $H \times W$ (rows $\times$ columns). We seek a binary wound mask:

$$
M(x, y, t) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if pixel } (x, y) \text{ is wound at time } t \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

and per-column edge functions:

$$
u(x, t) = \min\{y : M(x, y, t) = 1\} \quad \text{(upper edge)}
$$
$$
\ell(x, t) = \max\{y : M(x, y, t) = 1\} \quad \text{(lower edge)}
$$

where $y$ increases downward in image coordinates.

#### Wound Area

The total wound area at time $t$ is:

$$
A(t) = \sum_{x=0}^{W-1} \sum_{y=0}^{H-1} M(x, y, t) = \sum_{x=0}^{W-1} \max\bigl(0,\; \ell(x,t) - u(x,t) + 1\bigr)
$$

#### Biological Axiom (Monotonic Closure)

In a properly conducted scratch-wound assay, wound area is a monotonically non-increasing function of time:

$$
\boxed{A(t+1) \leq A(t) \quad \forall\; t \geq 0}
$$

We enforce this constraint at the per-column edge level (Section 2.3), which is strictly stronger than the aggregate area constraint.

### 2.2 Single-Frame Detection

The single-frame detector (`WoundDetector`) implements a seven-stage pipeline that transforms a raw grayscale image into a validated wound mask with smooth boundary curves. Each stage is described below.

#### Stage 1: Local Variance Texture Map

**Rationale.** Cell monolayers exhibit high spatial variance due to cell borders, nuclei, and phase-contrast halos. The wound channel, lacking cells, is comparatively smooth. We use local variance as a texture discriminator.

**Computation.** For a square window of side length $w$ (default $w = 15$), the local variance at each pixel is computed via the uniform-filter identity:

$$
\sigma^2(x, y) = \langle I^2 \rangle_w(x, y) - \bigl[\langle I \rangle_w(x, y)\bigr]^2
$$

where $\langle \cdot \rangle_w$ denotes the box-filter mean over a $w \times w$ neighborhood. Both terms are computed using `scipy.ndimage.uniform_filter`, yielding $O(HW)$ complexity independent of window size (the filter is separable and computed via running sums).

The raw variance map is post-smoothed with a separable Gaussian kernel ($\sigma_\text{smooth} = 2.0$ pixels, applied sequentially along rows and columns) to suppress high-frequency noise while preserving the wound--cell transition.

**Output:** $V(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, a smoothed local-variance map of shape $(H, W)$.

#### Stage 2: Y-Band Detection

**Rationale.** The wound is a horizontal band spanning (nearly) the full image width. Rather than searching the entire image, we first identify the vertical range $[y_\text{min}, y_\text{max}]$ containing the wound.

**Algorithm:**

1. Compute the row-averaged variance profile:
$$
\bar{V}(y) = \frac{1}{W} \sum_{x=0}^{W-1} V(x, y), \quad y \in \{0, \ldots, H-1\}
$$

2. Smooth with a 1-D Gaussian kernel ($\sigma_y = 10.0$ pixels) to obtain $\bar{V}_s(y)$.

3. Identify the wound center as the global minimum:
$$
y_c = \arg\min_y \bar{V}_s(y)
$$

4. Threshold $\bar{V}_s$ at the $p$-th percentile ($p = 35$) and extract the contiguous segment of sub-threshold rows that contains $y_c$.

5. Expand the band by a margin fraction $m = 0.15$:
$$
y_\text{min} \leftarrow \max(0,\; y_\text{min} - \lfloor m \cdot (y_\text{max} - y_\text{min}) \rfloor)
$$
$$
y_\text{max} \leftarrow \min(H-1,\; y_\text{max} + \lfloor m \cdot (y_\text{max} - y_\text{min}) \rfloor)
$$

**Output:** $(y_\text{min}, y_\text{max}, y_c)$ bounding the wound region vertically.

#### Stage 3: Column-Wise Edge Extraction

**Rationale.** Within the Y-band, we extract the upper and lower wound boundaries for each column by searching for the variance transition between cell (high $\sigma^2$) and wound (low $\sigma^2$) regions.

**Threshold.** The edge-detection threshold $\tau$ is derived from the cell regions flanking the wound:

$$
\tau = 0.5 \cdot \text{median}\bigl(\sigma^2_\text{cell}\bigr)
$$

where $\sigma^2_\text{cell}$ is the set of variance values in the rows above $y_\text{min}$ and below $y_\text{max}$. This adaptive threshold adjusts to the actual cell texture intensity of each image.

**Edge search (vectorized):**

- **Upper edge:** For each column $x$, search downward from $y_\text{min}$ toward $y_c$. The upper edge is the first row where variance drops below $\tau$:
$$
u_\text{raw}(x) = y_\text{min} + \min\{j : V(y_\text{min} + j,\, x) < \tau\}
$$
  Implemented as `np.argmax(below_threshold, axis=0)` on the boolean array $V[y_\text{min}:y_c, :] < \tau$, yielding the first `True` per column in $O(1)$ amortized per column.

- **Lower edge:** Search upward from $y_\text{max}$ toward $y_c$. The lower edge is the last row below threshold:
$$
\ell_\text{raw}(x) = y_\text{max} - \min\{j : V(y_\text{max} - j,\, x) < \tau\}
$$
  Implemented by flipping the search region vertically and applying `np.argmax`.

Columns where no transition is found default to $y_c$.

**Output:** $u_\text{raw}(x)$, $\ell_\text{raw}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^W$, raw per-column edge positions.

#### Stage 4: RANSAC Outlier Rejection

**Rationale.** Raw edges may contain outliers caused by imaging artifacts, debris, or partial cell detachment. We apply an iterative fit-and-reject scheme inspired by RANSAC.

**Algorithm (applied independently to each edge):**

```
RANSAC_EDGE(edge[0..W-1], degree=2, max_dev=25.0, iterations=3):
    edge_clean ← copy(edge)
    valid ← {x : edge[x] is not NaN}

    for iter = 1 to iterations:
        x_valid ← {x ∈ valid : edge_clean[x] is not NaN}
        if |x_valid| < 5:  break

        poly ← polyfit(x_valid, edge_clean[x_valid], degree)
        fitted ← polyval(poly, 0..W-1)
        residuals ← |edge_clean - fitted|

        outliers ← {x : residuals[x] > max_dev} ∩ valid
        edge_clean[outliers] ← fitted[outliers]

    return edge_clean
```

The polynomial degree (default 2, i.e., parabolic) captures the gentle curvature typical of scratch wounds while rejecting localized spikes. Three iterations suffice for convergence in practice.

**Complexity:** $O(W \cdot k)$ per edge, where $k$ is the number of iterations (typically 3).

**Output:** $u_\text{RANSAC}(x)$, $\ell_\text{RANSAC}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^W$, outlier-cleaned edges.

#### Stage 5: Savitzky--Golay Smoothing

**Rationale.** After outlier rejection, residual high-frequency jitter is smoothed with a Savitzky--Golay filter, which preserves edge shape (no phase distortion) while suppressing noise.

**Procedure:**

1. Fill remaining NaN values by linear interpolation across valid neighbors.
2. Apply `scipy.signal.savgol_filter` with window length $w_\text{sg} = 51$ and polynomial order $p_\text{sg} = 3$.

The window length is clamped to the array length and forced odd. The cubic polynomial order provides adequate smoothness without over-flattening genuine curvature.

**Output:** $u(x)$, $\ell(x) \in \mathbb{R}^W$, smooth final edges.

#### Stage 6: Mask Reconstruction

The binary mask is reconstructed from the smooth edges using vectorized broadcasting:

$$
M(x, y) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[u(x) \leq y \leq \ell(x)\bigr]
$$

Implementation:

```python
rows = np.arange(H)[:, None]              # shape (H, 1)
y_up = np.clip(upper.astype(int), 0, H-1) # shape (W,)
y_lo = np.clip(lower.astype(int), 0, H-1) # shape (W,)
mask = ((rows >= y_up[None, :]) & (rows <= y_lo[None, :])).astype(np.uint8)
```

**Complexity:** $O(HW)$, single pass, fully vectorized.

#### Stage 7: Geometric Validation

Four quality-control criteria are evaluated on the resulting mask:

| Criterion | Formula | Default Threshold | Rationale |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|-----------|
| Width coverage | $r_w = \frac{|\{x : \exists\, y \text{ s.t. } M(x,y)=1\}|}{W}$ | $r_w > 0.75$ | Wound must span most of image width |
| Aspect ratio | $\rho = \frac{x_\max - x_\min + 1}{y_\max - y_\min + 1}$ | $\rho > 1.5$ | Wound is wider than tall |
| Y-position | $\bar{y}_\text{frac} = \frac{\bar{y}_\text{wound}}{H}$ | $0.15 < \bar{y}_\text{frac} < 0.85$ | Wound is roughly centered |
| Thickness CV | $\text{CV}_\tau = \frac{\text{std}(\tau_x)}{\text{mean}(\tau_x)}$ where $\tau_x = \ell(x) - u(x) + 1$ | $\text{CV}_\tau < 0.60$ | Thickness is consistent across columns |

A detection passes QC if and only if all four criteria are satisfied simultaneously. Failures are logged with a diagnostic tag (e.g., `width_0.45_thickness_cv_0.72`).

#### Fallback: Constrained Region Growing

When the primary pipeline fails validation, an optional fallback strategy is activated:

1. Threshold the variance map at the 25th percentile to create a low-variance mask.
2. Place a thin horizontal seed (11 pixels tall) at $y_c$.
3. Grow the seed by intersection with the low-variance mask.
4. Apply morphological cleanup: hole filling (area threshold 500 px), small-object removal (min size 1000 px), and closing with a disk structuring element (radius 5).
5. Retain only the largest connected component.
6. Re-validate with the same four geometric criteria.

### 2.3 Temporal Constraint: Monotonic Closure

The `QuantificationSegmenter` wraps the single-frame `WoundDetector` with a stateful temporal integrator that enforces the biological axiom.

#### Formal Definition

Let $u_\text{det}(x, t)$ and $\ell_\text{det}(x, t)$ denote the raw detector output at time $t$. The constrained edges are defined recursively:

**Initialization** ($t = 0$):
$$
u(x, 0) = u_\text{det}(x, 0), \qquad \ell(x, 0) = \ell_\text{det}(x, 0)
$$

**Recursion** ($t > 0$):
$$
\boxed{u(x, t) = \max\bigl(u(x, t-1),\; u_\text{det}(x, t)\bigr)}
$$
$$
\boxed{\ell(x, t) = \min\bigl(\ell(x, t-1),\; \ell_\text{det}(x, t)\bigr)}
$$

**Interpretation.** The upper edge can only move *down* (increase in image coordinates) and the lower edge can only move *up* (decrease). This guarantees:

$$
\ell(x, t) - u(x, t) \leq \ell(x, t-1) - u(x, t-1) \quad \forall\, x, t
$$

and consequently $A(t) \leq A(t-1)$ for all $t > 0$.

#### Full Closure Handling

When the constrained edges meet or cross at a column ($u(x, t) \geq \ell(x, t)$), that column is declared fully closed with zero wound width. No artificial minimum gap is imposed, eliminating systematic area overestimation at late timepoints.

#### Algorithm

```
ENFORCE_MONOTONIC(u_det, l_det, u_prev, l_prev, H, W):
    u_constrained ← element-wise max(u_det, u_prev)
    l_constrained ← element-wise min(l_det, l_prev)

    y_up ← clip(round(u_constrained), 0, H-1)
    y_lo ← clip(round(l_constrained), 0, H-1)

    open_cols ← (y_up < y_lo)       // columns where wound is still open

    // Vectorized mask reconstruction via broadcasting
    rows ← [0, 1, ..., H-1]         // column vector (H, 1)
    mask ← (rows >= y_up) AND (rows <= y_lo) AND open_cols

    return u_constrained, l_constrained, mask
```

**Complexity:** $O(HW)$, fully vectorized, no Python loops.

#### Kalman-Filtered Constraint (Default)

When `use_kalman=True` (the default), the hard `max`/`min` constraint is replaced by a per-column 1-D Kalman filter that fuses the detector observation with a monotonic prediction, weighted by per-frame confidence.

**Process noise Q** represents expected edge movement per frame. It is estimated from the variance of frame-to-frame edge differences across clean trajectories (those passing t=0 QC). When no cached data is available (first run), a conservative default of $Q = 400$ px$^2$ is used. Q can also be set explicitly via `WoundDetectorConfig.kalman_Q`.

**Observation noise R** is computed per-column per-frame from three signals:
- **Variance contrast**: $R \propto 1 / \text{contrast}(x)^2$, where contrast is the ratio of cell variance to wound variance at column $x$. Low contrast (ambiguous boundary) → high R.
- **Edge jump**: $R \propto 1 + (\text{jump}(x) / \text{median\_jump})^2$. Large deviations from prediction → high R (likely artifact).
- **Detection method**: $R_{\text{fallback}} = 3 \times R_{\text{primary}}$.

The Kalman update per column:

$$K(x) = \frac{P_{\text{predicted}}}{P_{\text{predicted}} + R(x,t)}, \qquad \hat{u}(x,t) = u(x, t-1) + K(x) \cdot \bigl(u_{\text{det}}(x,t) - u(x,t-1)\bigr)$$

A hard safety net is applied after the Kalman blend to guarantee the biological invariant: $u(x,t) = \max(\hat{u}(x,t),\; u(x,t-1))$.

**Key advantage:** A spurious inward edge (debris, bubble) receives high R → K ≈ 0 → the filter trusts the monotonic prediction. Once the artifact clears, the next clean frame is trusted again. The hard constraint permanently locks spurious edges; the Kalman filter attenuates them.

To reproduce legacy (hard-constraint-only) results, set `use_kalman=False` in `WoundDetectorConfig`.

#### State Management

The segmenter maintains three state vectors per trajectory:

| State | Description | Updated at |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| `t0_upper`, `t0_lower`, `t0_mask` | Reference frame (baseline) | $t = 0$ only |
| `prev_upper`, `prev_lower`, `prev_mask` | Previous frame | Every $t$ |
| `_t` | Current time counter | Every call |

A call to `reset()` clears all state, preparing the segmenter for a new biological sample. This is essential: the monotonic constraint is defined *within* a trajectory, not across different samples.

### 2.4 Trajectory Processing Pipeline

#### Per-Sample Workflow

For each biological sample (identified by a unique `sample_name` $\times$ `experiment` $\times$ `exposure` tuple):

1. **Image discovery.** Sort image files by acquisition time parsed from filenames (pattern: `DDdHHhMMm`).
2. **Sequential segmentation.** Feed images to `QuantificationSegmenter.segment()` in temporal order. The segmenter auto-increments $t$ and applies the monotonic constraint for $t > 0$.
3. **Result serialization.** Save per-frame results (image, mask, edges, area, QC flags) to `experiment.pkl` (full data) and `summary.csv` (lightweight table).
4. **Trajectory visualization.** Generate a two-panel plot: absolute wound area and normalized area (% of $A(0)$) vs.\ time.

#### Parallel Execution

Independent samples are processed in parallel using Python's `concurrent.futures.ProcessPoolExecutor`. The number of worker processes is explicitly controlled ($n_\text{workers}$, default 4, capped at the number of available CPU cores). Each worker instantiates its own `QuantificationSegmenter` with independent state, ensuring no shared mutable data.

#### Legacy Metadata Integration

The pipeline reads experiment metadata from Excel tables (`{exposure}_{experiment}_sample_file.xlsx`) containing sample identifiers, filenames, and acquisition timestamps. Segmentation results are merged back into this table via an inner join on filename, producing a unified `final_legacy_table.xlsx` with both metadata and quantitative wound measurements.

### 2.5 Quality Control

Quality control operates at three levels, from automated single-frame checks to optional human curation.

#### Level 1: Single-Frame Geometric Validation

Applied automatically to every frame by `WoundDetector._validate_wound_geometry()`. The four criteria (Section 2.2, Stage 7) flag frames with implausible geometry. Frames failing QC are either reprocessed via the fallback detector or flagged for downstream exclusion.

#### Level 2: Baseline (t = 0) Distributional Analysis

The baseline mask undergoes additional scrutiny via `verify_wound_by_distribution()`, which evaluates two projection profiles:

**Y-Profile (Row Projection).** The row-wise sum of the mask, smoothed with a Gaussian kernel ($\sigma = 15$), should exhibit exactly one prominent peak (the wound band). The peak center must lie within $\pm 15\%$ of the mask centroid.

Formally, let $P_y(y) = \sum_x M(x, y, 0)$. We compute:
$$
P_{y,s}(y) = (P_y * G_\sigma)(y)
$$

and require: (a) exactly one peak with prominence $> 0.15 \cdot \max(P_{y,s})$; (b) peak offset $< 0.15 \cdot H$ from the mask centroid.

**X-Profile (Column Projection).** The column-wise sum $P_x(x) = \sum_y M(x, y, 0)$, normalized to $[0, 1]$, should be roughly rectangular (flat across the wound width). Two criteria:

- Skewness: $|\text{skew}(P_x)| < 0.8$
- Central band flatness: $\text{std}(P_x[0.25W : 0.75W]) < 0.15$

A trajectory is retained only if both Y and X criteria pass.

#### Level 3: Manual Curation (Optional)

For high-stakes analyses, the pipeline supports a manual annotation workflow. Baseline images are exported to an `annotation_sheet.xlsx` for human review. A `keep` column flags samples for inclusion/exclusion, and downstream filtering respects these annotations.

### 2.6 Downstream Metrics

#### Edge Displacement and Closure Speed

Given edges at $t = 0$ and a target time $t_T$, per-edge displacements are:

$$
\Delta u(x) = u(x, t_T) - u(x, 0) \qquad \text{(upper edge, positive = moved down)}
$$
$$
\Delta \ell(x) = \ell(x, 0) - \ell(x, t_T) \qquad \text{(lower edge, positive = moved up)}
$$

Speeds are computed as mean absolute displacements divided by elapsed time:

$$
v_\text{upper} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_u|} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{V}_u} \frac{|\Delta u(x)|}{t_T}, \qquad v_\text{lower} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_\ell|} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{V}_\ell} \frac{|\Delta \ell(x)|}{t_T}
$$

where $\mathcal{V}_u$, $\mathcal{V}_\ell$ are the sets of columns with valid (non-NaN) edges at both timepoints. The mean closure speed is:

$$
v_\text{mean} = \frac{v_\text{upper} + v_\text{lower}}{2}
$$

#### Wound Closing Determination

A trajectory is classified as "closing" if a minimum fraction of valid columns ($f_\text{min} = 0.6$) show inward edge motion at both boundaries:

$$
\text{is\_closing} = \biggl(\frac{|\{x \in \mathcal{V}_u : \Delta u(x) > 0\}|}{|\mathcal{V}_u|} \geq f_\text{min}\biggr) \;\wedge\; \biggl(\frac{|\{x \in \mathcal{V}_\ell : \Delta \ell(x) > 0\}|}{|\mathcal{V}_\ell|} \geq f_\text{min}\biggr)
$$

#### Analysis Modes

Two complementary analysis modes are provided:

- **Cross-sectional.** Compare wound metrics at a single target timepoint across experimental conditions (e.g., drug vs.\ control at $t = 20$ h).
- **Time-series.** Track wound metrics at all available timepoints, enabling kinetic curve fitting and time-to-closure estimation.

#### Computational Complexity

| Stage | Operation | Complexity | Notes |
|-------|-----------|------------|-------|
| 1. Local variance | Uniform filter (separable) | $O(HW)$ | Independent of window size |
| 2. Y-band | 1-D profile + argmin | $O(H + W)$ | Negligible |
| 3. Edge extraction | Boolean argmax, vectorized | $O(HW)$ | No Python loops |
| 4. RANSAC | Polyfit $\times$ iterations | $O(Wk)$ | $k = 3$ iterations |
| 5. Savgol | 1-D filter | $O(W)$ | Single pass |
| 6. Mask rebuild | Broadcasting | $O(HW)$ | Single vectorized operation |
| 7. Validation | Summary statistics | $O(HW)$ | Column sums + aggregation |
| Monotonic constraint | Element-wise max/min | $O(W)$ | Per-column, vectorized |
| Mask from constraint | Broadcasting | $O(HW)$ | Single vectorized operation |

**Total per frame:** $O(HW)$, dominated by the variance computation and mask reconstruction. For a typical $1024 \times 1280$ image, this corresponds to $\sim 1.3 \times 10^6$ operations per stage.

#### Vectorization Strategy

All per-column operations that would naively require Python-level `for` loops are implemented using NumPy broadcasting and fancy indexing. Specifically:

- Edge extraction (Stage 3): `np.argmax` on 2-D boolean arrays replaces per-column search loops.
- Mask reconstruction (Stage 6): rank-broadcasting `rows[:, None] >= edges[None, :]` produces the full $(H, W)$ mask in a single operation.
- Monotonic constraint: element-wise `np.maximum` / `np.minimum` on 1-D edge arrays.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Advantages

The Quantification pipeline offers several methodological advances over existing tools:

**No training data.** The variance-based detector exploits a fundamental physical property of phase-contrast imaging (cells scatter light; the empty wound channel does not) rather than learned features. This eliminates the need for annotated training sets and avoids the domain-shift problem inherent in deep-learning segmenters.

**Biologically grounded constraint.** The monotonic closure axiom is not merely a regularizer; it encodes a physical law of the assay system. This produces strictly non-increasing area curves that are directly interpretable without post-hoc smoothing.

**Computational efficiency.** Full vectorization ensures that per-frame processing is dominated by array operations, not Python overhead. The $O(HW)$ scaling enables real-time processing of standard microscopy images.

**Modular design.** The single-frame detector and temporal integrator are cleanly separated. The detector can be used independently for snapshot analyses, while the integrator can, in principle, wrap any detector that produces per-column edge arrays.

### 4.2 Limitations

Several known limitations warrant discussion:

**Irreversible per-column constraint (W8).** If the detector produces a spurious inward edge at time $t$ (due to debris, bubble, or focus artifact), the hard monotonic constraint permanently propagates that error to all subsequent frames for the affected columns. This has been mitigated by the Kalman-filtered constraint (default since v2), which attenuates spurious observations by assigning them high observation noise. The hard safety net preserves the biological invariant while the Kalman blend prevents permanent error lock-in. The hard constraint remains available via `use_kalman=False` for reproducibility of legacy results.

**Fixed threshold heuristic (W3).** The edge-detection threshold ($\tau = 0.5 \cdot \text{median}(\sigma^2_\text{cell})$) assumes unimodal cell variance and a clean cell--wound boundary. Images with heterogeneous cell populations or illumination gradients may require adaptive thresholding.

**Integer-pixel edges (W9).** Edges are extracted at integer pixel resolution. For thin wounds approaching closure, the 1-pixel quantization error represents a significant fraction of the remaining width. Sub-pixel edge localization (e.g., parabolic interpolation of the variance profile around the threshold crossing) would improve precision.

**No uncertainty quantification (W13).** The pipeline produces point estimates of wound area without confidence intervals. Bootstrapping area estimates under small edge perturbations would provide uncertainty bounds.

**No physical calibration (W14).** All measurements are in pixel units. Incorporating a pixel-to-micrometer conversion factor (from microscope metadata or a calibration slide) would yield physically meaningful areas and speeds.

### 4.3 Future Directions

Planned improvements include: (1) adaptive per-column thresholding using local statistics or Otsu's method; (2) sub-pixel edge extraction via parabolic fitting; (3) ~~a Kalman filter combining the detector output with the monotonic prediction~~ (implemented; see Section 2.3); (4) bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification; and (5) integration of pixel-size metadata for calibrated measurements.

---

## 5. Conclusion

We have presented Quantification, a fully automated pipeline for scratch-wound healing quantification that combines variance-based single-frame detection with a biologically motivated monotonic closure constraint. The pipeline requires no training data, processes standard phase-contrast microscopy images in linear time, and produces smooth, strictly non-increasing wound area trajectories. A multi-tier quality-control system ensures robust performance, while parallel trajectory processing enables scalable analysis of high-content experiments. The mathematical formulation is presented in full, all parameters are documented, and the implementation is released as open-source Python code. We anticipate that Quantification will reduce the time and subjectivity currently associated with scratch-wound quantification, enabling researchers to focus on biological interpretation rather than manual measurement.
